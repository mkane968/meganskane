ENG 3121/6122: Digital Text Methods

Instructor: Dr. Megan Kane

Semester: Fall 2026

Meeting Time: Tuesdays, 5–7:30 p.m.

Location: TBD

This course introduces computational approaches to textual analysis using large collections of texts, or corpora. You will learn how to assemble and document a corpus, develop research questions suited to large-scale analysis, and use digital methods to identify patterns of style, structure, and meaning across texts. Along the way, you will consider how corpus analysis makes new kinds of textual inquiry possible—particularly questions that emerge from patterns distributed across dozens or hundreds of texts.

Corpora for Analysis

For your final project, you will select texts from one of the following preapproved corpora:

  • CLiC Fiction Corpora (opens in a new tab): nineteenth-century British novels and children’s literature
  • Folger Shakespeare Corpus (opens in a new tab): Shakespeare’s plays and poetry
  • Project Gutenberg Poetry Corpus (opens in a new tab): a large collection of public-domain English-language poetry
  • Michigan Corpus of Upper-Level Student Papers (opens in a new tab): a large collection of high-scoring undergraduate and graduate student writing across genres and disciplines

Rather than analyzing an entire collection as given, you will use available texts and metadata to construct a focused subcorpus suited to an original research question. The subcorpus must include at least one meaningful point of comparison—such as genre, historical period, authorship, geographic region, character group, or another relevant category—and contain enough texts in each group to support responsible comparison.

Major Assignments
  • Data Paper (15%): A 1,000-word report documenting the corpus or subcorpus constructed for the final project, including its sources, scope, metadata, selection criteria, and at least one meaningful point of comparison.
  • Project Proposal (10%): A 3–4-page proposal outlining the research question, corpus, comparison groups, primary computational method, relevant scholarship, potential limitations, and completion plan.
  • Final Paper (Case Study) (40%): A corpus-based case study using one primary computational method and supporting interpretive analysis, submitted with annotated Python notebooks. The paper is 7–10 pages for undergraduate students and 10–12 pages for graduate students.
  • Presentation (5%): A 3–5-minute presentation introducing the research question, corpus, method, and most important finding, including one clear visualization.
